How to Find Low Competition Keywords for Listings

Published 2026-11-10

TL;DR: Find low competition keywords for listings by starting with buyer intent, building phrases from the product itself, narrowing broad terms into specific combinations, checking the search results for real competition, and keeping only keywords that fit Etsy’s title rules and the listing’s actual details.

Start with buyer intent, not just search volume

Low competition keywords for listings often reflect shopper intent and can be more specific than broad terms. A specific intent, such as a product plus a use case, is often easier to stand out in than a broad term that pulls in everything loosely related.

A good starting point is to match keyword ideas to the questions buyers are already asking. If you want a clean companion to that approach, how to write a product description that answers buyer questions keeps the focus on shopper intent instead of keyword clutter.

Build a seed list from the listing itself

Start with the item you actually sell. The easiest seed list comes from the listing itself, not from a random keyword buffet.

Pull words and phrases from:

  • the product name
  • material
  • use case
  • audience
  • style
  • occasion
  • problem solved

Then turn those into short and long phrase variations. For example, if the item is a candle, the seed ideas might come from the scent type, the room it is meant for, the buyer it suits, or the mood it creates. Keep it natural. If the phrase would sound weird spoken out loud, it will probably feel weird in a title too.

The goal is not to collect every possible phrase. It is to find terms a listing can realistically rank for because they genuinely describe the item.

Expand into narrower, more searchable phrases

Once you have seeds, make them more specific by combining the product with other details:

  • product plus use
  • product plus audience
  • product plus style
  • product plus occasion

So instead of a broad term, you get something that points to a narrower buyer intent. That is often the better starting point for a small seller, because it gives searchers less room to wander off and more reason to click the exact thing you sell.

A more precise phrase is not automatically better, but it is often less crowded and easier to connect to one clear listing.

Check whether a keyword is actually “low competition”

You can do a basic reality check by looking at the search results and the kinds of listings that appear.

Look at:

  • how many listings are competing for the phrase
  • whether the results are dominated by very similar sellers or very large sellers
  • whether the phrase is so broad that it is not really listing-specific

If a search result page is full of near-identical products from major shops, that can suggest the phrase may be more competitive. If the term is so broad that it could apply to a dozen different product types, it is probably not the cleanest target for a single listing.

Use Etsy’s own title rules to screen out bad keyword ideas

Some phrases may be decent search terms and still be lousy title candidates. If a phrase is too long, awkward, or repetitive, it can make the title read like a keyword casserole, which nobody ordered.

Use Etsy’s title guidance to filter out those bad fits. Check the current guidance before you lock anything in.

Worked example: turning a broad product idea into lower-competition listing phrases

Here is a hypothetical example. Say the item is a linen laptop sleeve.

A broad starting point might be:

  • laptop sleeve

Then narrow it into candidates like:

  • linen laptop sleeve
  • laptop sleeve for work bag
  • minimalist linen laptop sleeve
  • linen laptop sleeve for women
  • linen laptop sleeve for travel

Which one is most likely to be less competitive? Usually the more specific phrase, such as minimalist linen laptop sleeve or linen laptop sleeve for travel, because it combines the product with a style or use case. That makes the intent clearer and usually trims away some of the broader competition.

The tradeoff is simple. If the phrase is too narrow, it may not match enough real shoppers. The sweet spot is a phrase that is specific, natural, and still true to the item.

Prioritize phrases you can support across the listing

The best low competition keywords are the ones you can use naturally in the title, tags, and description without stuffing the same phrase into every available corner.

If a phrase only fits awkwardly, or it describes a different product intent, drop it. Even if it looks easy to rank for, it is not useful if the listing cannot support it honestly and clearly.

This is where consistency matters. Your title, tags, and description should all point to the same item and the same buyer intent.

What to do next after you pick the keyword set

Once you have a keyword set, do this:

  1. Choose one primary phrase.
  2. Add a few close variants.
  3. Put the most important phrase early in the title.
  4. Keep the rest aligned with the listing’s actual details.

If your keyword ideas are drifting into different buyer intents rather than true variations of the same product, when a seller lists a product with multiple variants, what should be done will help you decide whether you need one listing or more than one.

The short version is this: start with buyer intent, build from the item itself, narrow the phrase until it feels specific enough to rank, then make sure the keyword still fits the actual listing without turning the title into word salad.
